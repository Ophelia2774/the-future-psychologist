Issues & Debates

Nature vs Nurture

NatureNurture
Behaviour is determined by biological factors such as genes, hormones, and brain structure. We are born with these characteristics.Behaviour is shaped by environment and experiences after birth, such as upbringing, culture, and learning.

(Nature vs Nurture in Bandura et al.)

  • Nature refers to behaviours that are genetically encoded and present from birth. In Bandura's study, boys showed significantly more physical aggression than girls, which could be explained by boys naturally having higher levels of testosterone, suggesting aggression has a biological basis.
  • Nurture refers to behaviours learned through experiences and the environment after birth. In Bandura's study, children who observed an aggressive model were more likely to imitate aggressive behaviour compared to those who observed a non-aggressive model, despite all participants being matched on pre-existing aggression levels — showing that aggression was learned through observation.

Individual vs Situational

IndividualSituational
Behaviour is explained by internal factors such as personality or cognitive ability.Behaviour is explained by external factors such as social pressure, environment, or context.

(Individual vs Situational in Dement & Kleitman)

  • The individual explanation refers to behaviours caused by factors within the person. In Dement and Kleitman, all dreams reported were unique — for example, one participant dreamed of throwing tomatoes — suggesting that individual personality is reflected in dream content.
  • The situational explanation refers to behaviours caused by factors in the external environment. In Dement and Kleitman, participants slept in a laboratory with an EEG attached, which may have affected their ability to recall dreams or accurately estimate time spent in REM sleep.

(Individual vs Situational in Piliavin et al.)

  • The individual explaination suggests that behaviour is caused by factors within the person. For example, males were far more likely to help than females (90% of first helpers were male), and same-race helping was more common with drunk victims — suggesting that personal characteristics such as gender and race also played a role in bystander behaviour.
  • The situational explanation suggests that behaviour is caused by external factors in the environment. Piliavin's study supports this as the type of victim (ill vs drunk) affected how likely bystanders were to help, and being trapped on the train meant people could not leave the situation — suggesting the environment, not personal characteristics, was the key factor driving helping behaviour.

The use of children in psychological research

  • Children are a vulnerable group, raising concerns about whether they can fully understand and consent to participation in psychological research.
  • Researchers have a responsibility to protect children from harm, ensuring that studies do not cause unnecessary distress or negatively affect their development.
  • Despite ethical concerns, research involving children is essential for understanding cognitive, social, and emotional development and improving educational and psychological support.

Cultural differences

  • Cultural differences refer to the ways people's thoughts, emotions, and behaviours can vary across different cultures.
  • Psychologists study these differences to understand whether certain behaviours are universal or influenced by cultural values and beliefs.
  • Recognising cultural differences helps reduce bias and promotes a more accurate understanding of human behaviour.

Reductionism vs Holism

ReductionismHolism
Behaviour is explained by breaking it down into smaller, simpler components. e.g. explaining behaviour through just one hormone or brain region.Behaviour is best understood by looking at the whole person and all factors together.

Determinism vs Free-will

DeterminismFree-will
The view that human behaviour is controlled by factors such as biology, upbringing, or the environment, meaning people have limited control over their actions.The view that individuals can make their own choices and have control over their behaviour, regardless of external influences.

Idiographic vs Nomothetic

IdiographicNomothetic
Focuses on understanding the unique experiences, thoughts, and behaviours of individuals. It suggests that behaviour is best understood by studying each person in detail.Focuses on identifying general laws and patterns of behaviour that apply to large groups of people. It aims to make predictions about human behaviour through scientific research.

Research Methods

Methods, Strengths & Weaknesses

🧪 Experiments

An experiment manipulates an independent variable (IV) to measure its effect on a dependent variable (DV), while controlling extraneous variables.

Types

TypeDescriptionExample
LaboratoryConducted in a controlled environment. Researcher controls all variablesAndrade (doodling)
FieldConducted in a natural setting. IV manipulated but environment is realPiliavin et al. (subway)
Natural (not an actual experiment)An experiment where the IV occurs naturally and is not manipulated by the researcher, but the DV is still measuredHodges and Tizard (1989)

Experimental Designs

DesignDescription✓ Strengths✗ Weaknesses
Independent MeasuresDifferent participants in each conditionNo order effects; low demand characteristicsParticipant variables possible - random allocation needed; more participants are required
Repeated MeasuresSame participants in all conditionsNo participant variables; fewer participants are neededOrder effects possible - counterbalancing needed; demand characteristics possible
Matched PairsPairs matched on key variables, one in each conditionReduces individual differencesHard to match perfectly; time-consuming

Conditions

  • Experimental condition - The group or situation where the IV is present, meaning participants are exposed to the factor being tested.
  • Control condition - The group or situation where the IV is absent, used as a baseline for comparison to see whether the experimental condition made a difference.

In experiment...

  • ... you must decide how to operationalize the IV so that different conditions are clear & valid
  • ... the DV must be measured in a consistent, reliable way
  • ... you need to be able to justify your choices of IV & DV
  • ... you will also need to be able to decide what controls are appropriate to use & to suggest how these can be implemented
✓ Strengths
  • High control over variables — can establish cause and effect
  • Replicable — others can repeat to check reliability
  • Quantitative data — objective and easy to analyse
✗ Weaknesses
  • Lab: low ecological validity — artificial setting
  • Demand characteristics — participants guess aim and alter behaviour
  • Field: less control, harder to replicate
  • Ethical issues possible (deception, consent)

📋 Self-Reports

Participants report their own thoughts, feelings or behaviours. Includes questionnaires and interviews.

Questionnaires

Technique: Offline (paper & pencil) or Online (postal)

Postal questionnaires: a data collection method in which questionnaires are sent to participants by mail, completed independently, and returned to the researchers

  • Open questions: Free-form answers — rich, in-depth, qualitative data; harder to analyse
  • Closed questions: Fixed responses (e.g. Likert scale, Rating scale) — less depth; simple to work out average & define pattern
    • Likert scales: questions asking whether the participant likes or dislikes something
    • Rating scales: questions that require the participant to represent their answer on the numerical scale provided
✓ Strengths
  • Quick and cheap to collect large amounts of data
  • Anonymous - encouraging honest answers
  • Standardised questions - easy to replicate
  • Closed questions - easy to analyse
✗ Weaknesses
  • Social desirability bias — participants give "good" answers
  • Participants may misunderstand questions
  • Closed questions - limit responses and miss detail
  • Cannot follow up or clarify answers

Interviews

Technique: Face-to-face or telephone

TypeDescription
StructuredSame set questions for all; consistent, comparable data; less flexible - highly standardised
Semi-structuredCore questions + follow-ups; balance of depth and consistency; more flexible
UnstructuredFree-flowing conversation; in-depth; not replicable
✓ Strengths
  • Can ask follow-up questions to get detailed, in-depth answers
  • Misunderstandings can be clarified on the spot
  • Produces rich qualitative data
  • More flexible than questionnaires
✗ Weaknesses
  • Time consuming and expensive
  • Interviewer bias may influence participants' answers
  • Participants may give socially desirable answers
  • Difficult to replicate due to lack of standardisation
  • Small samples, limiting generalisation

🔍 Case Studies

An in-depth investigation of a single participant or unit (individual, group, event). Uses multiple data collection techniques (interviews, observations, psychometric tests).

Key features: single participant/unit; studied in great detail; often longitudinal; uses triangulation.

Triangulation: Using multiple methods to cross-check findings and increase validity.

✓ Strengths
  • Collect rich, in-depth (qualitative) data
  • Useful for studying rare or unique cases that cannot be studied any other way
  • Generate new hypotheses for future research
  • Triangulation improves validity
✗ Weaknesses
  • Only 1 participant - low generalisability
  • Researcher bias in interpretation
  • Relies heavily on self-report & memory - unreliable
  • Not replicable
  • Ethical issues — confidentiality & privacy

👁️ Observations

Systematically watching and recording behaviour. Can be classified on multiple dimensions:

DimensionOption AOption B
AwarenessOvert — participants know they're observedCovert — participants unaware
InvolvementParticipant — observer joins groupNon-participant — observer stays separate
SettingNaturalistic — natural environmentControlled — lab/artificial setting
StructureStructured — behavioural checklist/categoriesUnstructured — free-form notes

Inter-observer reliability

The degree to which different observers give consistent scores or judgments when measuring the same behaviour. High agreement = high reliability.

✓ Strengths
  • Captures natural behaviour in real settings - high ecological validity
  • No reliance on self-report - reduce social desirability bias
  • Can study behaviours that participants may not be able to verbally report
  • Useful for studying children or animals who cannot complete questionnaires
✗ Weaknesses
  • Observer bias - affect how behaviour is interpreted and recorded
  • Lack of control over extraneous variables
  • Cannot determine the cause of behaviour
  • Ethical issues - informed consent; especially covert
  • Difficult to replicate due to uncontrolled conditions

📈 Correlations

A correlational study measures the relationship between two co-variables without manipulating either. Does NOT establish cause and effect.

TypeDescriptionExample
PositiveBoth variables increase togetherMore study time → higher grades
NegativeAs one increases, the other decreasesMore stress → less sleep
ZeroNo relationship between variablesShoe size and IQ

Scatter graph is used to display correlational data. The correlation coefficient (r) ranges from −1 to +1; closer to ±1 = stronger relationship.

For exam planning: Must identify two co-variables, operational measure of each, and predicted nature of relationship.
✓ Strengths
  • Allows researchers to measure the relationship between two variables
  • Useful when it would be unethical or impractical to conduct an experiment
  • Can identify patterns and trends in data
  • Provides a starting point for further experimental research
✗ Weaknesses
  • Cannot establish cause and effect, only a relationship between variables
  • Can be misinterpreted as showing causation when it does not
  • Limited to measuring variables that can be quantified

⏳ Longitudinal Studies

A study that follows the same participants over an extended period of time, testing them repeatedly at intervals. Can use any research method (experiment, observation, etc.) with a longitudinal design.

✓ Strengths
  • Tracks changes in behaviour or development over time
  • Reduces participant variables as the same people are studied throughout
  • Provides rich, detailed data on long-term patterns and trends
✗ Weaknesses
  • Sample attrition — participants drop out over time = reduce generalisability
  • Practice (order) effects — repeated testing affects performance
  • Expensive & time-consuming
  • Changes in the research team over time may affect consistency

📝 Psychometric tests

A standardized assessment used to measure psychological characteristics such as intelligence, personality, abilities, attitudes, or mental traits.

✓ Strengths
  • Standardised – all participants complete the same test under the same conditions, making results easier to compare
  • Objective – uses fixed scoring systems, reducing researcher bias
  • Reliable – can produce consistent results when administered correctly
  • Quantitative data – provides numerical scores that are easy to analyse statistically
✗ Weaknesses
  • May lack validity – the test may not accurately measure what it claims to measure
  • Cultural bias – some tests may be more suitable for certain cultures than others
  • Reductionist – complex human characteristics may be oversimplified into a score
  • Social desirability bias – in personality tests, participants may give answers they think are more acceptable rather than truthful
  • Lack of depth – numerical scores may not fully explain an individual's behaviour or experiences

Variables & Controls

Types of Variables

VariableDefinition
IV (Independent)The variable that the researcher changes or manipulates to see its effect
DV (Dependent)The variable that is measured to see if it changes because of the IV
Extraneous variablesAny variable other than IV that could affect DV = must be controlled
Uncontrolled variables (Extraneous)Extraneous variable that varies randomly with IV (affecting all levels of IV)
Confounding variables (Extraneous)Extraneous variable that varies systematically with IV (affecting only 1 level of IV) = threatens internal validity
Participant variables (Confounding)Caused by individual differences between participants (age, mood, ability) [INTERNAL]
Situational variables (Confounding)Caused by an aspect of the environment [EXTERNAL]

Operationalisation

Defining variables in measurable, precise terms. E.g., "aggression" operationalised as "number of times a child hits a Bobo doll in 5 minutes."

Controlling of Variables

ControlsDefinition
Pilot studyA practical version of the experiment to spot mistakes & fix them early, helps identify uncontrolled variables early so they can be controlled
StandardizationGive all participants the same instructions & use the same preocedure/ equipment so everyone is treat equally, ensuring validity & reliability
Random allocation (independent measures design)Randomly assign participants to conditions so participant variables are distributed fairly [RCTs - Randomised Control Trials]
Counterbalancing (repeated measures design)Vary the order of conditions for different participants to cancel out practice or fatigue effects (order effects)
Matching participants (matched pairs design)Pair participants with similar characteristics to reduce participant variable differences
RandomizationRandomly order materials (e.g. word lists, questions) so bias doesn't affect results
Control groups (experiment)Use a comparision group that doesn't receive the IV to see if the IV really causes the change in DV

Aim & Hypothesis

Aim:

A clear statement of the purpose of a study, describing what the researcher intends to investigate.

(It's written before the experiment is carried out & doesn't predict outcomes)

Hypothesis:

A testable statement predicting the outcome of a study or the relationship between variables.

  • Null hypothesis (H₀): NO significant difference/relationship; any difference is due to chance
  • Alternative hypothesis (H₁): There IS a significant difference/relationship
    1. Directional (one-tailed): Predicts direction of effect (e.g., "Group A will score higher than Group B")
    2. Non-directional (two-tailed): Predicts a difference but not direction (e.g., "There will be a significant difference")

Validity & Reliability

Validity

The extent to which the researchers is testing what they claim to be testing

ConceptDefinition
Internal validityThe extent to which a study accurately shows a cause-and-effect relationship between variables, without influence from other confounding factors
Social desirability biasWhen participants give answers they think are socially acceptable or favourable, rather than completely truthful
Demand characteristicsCues in a study that signal to participants what behaviour is expected, which may cause them to change their behaviour
Order effectsPractice effects (improve) or fatigue effects (worsen) from repeated testing
External validityThe extent to which the results of a study can be applied to other situations, populations, or settings outside the study
Temporal validityThe extent to which research findings remain relevant and applicable over time. If behaviour, society, or cultural norms change, older findings may no longer accurately reflect current behaviour
Ecological validityThe extent to which a study’s findings reflect real-life behaviour in natural settings
Mundane realismThe degree to which the tasks or activities in an experiment resemble real-world activities
GeneralisabilityThe extent to which the findings from a sample can be applied to the wider population or different contexts
ObjectivityWhen research or observations are free from personal bias and based on observable, measurable facts
SubjectivityWhen interpretations or observations are influenced by personal opinions, feelings, or biases

Reliability

The extent to which a procedure, task or measure is consistent

ConceptDefinition
Internal reliabilityThe extent to which items or measurements within a test or study produce consistent results with each other.
External reliabilityThe extent to which a study produces consistent results when it is repeated, either over time or by different researchers.
Inter-rater reliabilityThe degree to which different observers or raters give consistent scores or judgments when measuring the same behaviour or outcome.

Ethical guidelines

Ethical Considerations For Human Participants

PrincipleExplanation
Informed consentParticipants must be given sufficient information about the study’s purpose, risks, and procedures to make a conscious decision whether they want to participate or not.
Protection from harmParticipants should not be exposed to any physical or psychological risks greater than those encountered in normal daily life.
Right to withdrawParticipants are allowed to leave the study at any time and have their data removed at any point without penalty or pressure.
DeceptionParticipants should not be deliberately misinformed. If unavoidable for the study's validity, distress must be minimized.
DebriefingA post-study explanation revealing the true aim & consequences of the study, ensuring participants leave in the same psychological state that they arrived in.
PrivacyAvoiding the invasion of a participant's personal physical or psychological space without their explicit knowledge or comfort.
ConfidentialityA requirement to keep all participant data, results, and personal information secure and unreleased outside the research team.

Ethical Considerations For Animal Participants

Principle Explanation
Replacement Researchers should consider replacing animal experiments with alternatives, such as videos from previous studies or computer simulations.
Species The chosen species should be the least likely to suffer pain or distress. Consider captive breeding, prior experimentation experience, and sentience (ability to think and feel).
Number of Animals Only the minimum number needed for valid and reliable results should be used. Achieved through pilot studies, reliable DV measures, and good experimental design.
Procedures Research must follow legal requirements and guidelines. Animals' experience should be as normal and positive as possible within the constraints of the study.
Pain, Suffering & Distress Research causing death, disease, pain, or psychological distress should be avoided. Costs to animals must be justified by scientific benefit (Bateson's cube, 1986).
Housing Caging should suit the species' social behaviour. Animals need sufficient space, food, and water. Environments should replicate survival-relevant aspects, not just appear visually appealing.
Reward, Deprivation & Aversive Stimuli Normal feeding and drinking patterns should be considered. Preferred food should be used as reward where possible. Alternatives to deprivation and aversive stimuli should be used wherever possible.

Biological Approach

Behaviour explained through brain, hormones, genetics & evolution

Core Assumptions

1. Human behaviours & cognitions can be explained in terms of brain function & the effect of hormones/genes.

2. The similarities & differences seen in human behaviour can be understood in term of internal (biological) factors & how they interact with external factors.

Dement & Kleitman (1957) — Sleep & Dreams
Lab experiment - Repeated measures design Correlation Opportunity sampling 9 (7M/2F) 1957
▼
Background

Aserinsky and Kleitman (1955) discovered that sleep consists of alternating REM and non-REM stages, and that vivid dreaming is strongly associated with REM sleep — laying the foundation for modern sleep science.

Psychology being investigated
  • Sleep is a natural recurring state of rest where the body and brain are less conscious of the outside world. The brain remains active during sleep, cycling through different stages throughout the night.
  • Dream is a subjective experience of imagery whilst we are asleep.
  • Ultradian rhythms are biological cycles that repeat multiple times within a single day. In sleep, the brain cycles through REM and NREM stages roughly every 90 minutes.
  • REM (rapid eye movement) sleep is a phase of sleep where a person eyes flicker and they can breathe but all other muscles are paralysed.
  • nREM (non-rapid eye movement) sleep are the other 4 stages of sleep and they differ in brain activity.
  • EEG (electroencephalography) records the brain’s electrical activity (amplitude and frequency of brain waves) using electrodes placed on the scalp.
  • EOG (electrooculography) records electrical signals generated by eye movements using electrodes placed around the eyes.
Aim

1. To test whether dream recall differs between REM & nREM sleep

2. To investigate whether their is a positive correletion between subjective estimates of dream duration & the length of REM period

3. To test whether eye movement patterns are related to dream content

Sample
  • 9 adult participants (7 males, 2 females)
  • 5 participants were studied intensively (6-17 nights with 50-77 awakenings)
  • Data gathered from other 4 were minimal (1-2 nights with 4-10 awakenings) to confirm the results
Procedure + Results

(GENERAL PROCEDURE)

  • Participants reported to the lab a little earlier than their usual bedtime
  • They were instructed to eat normally but to abstrain from alcoholic or caffeine - containing beverages on the day of the experiment
  • Participants slept in a quiet, dark room with EEG electrodes attached to their scalps and around their eyes
  • All electrode lead wires were further attached to top of the head.
  • Participants were woken by an ordinary doorbell (placed near the bed) that was loud enough to rouse them from any sleep stage
  • The participant had to speak into a recording device (next to the bed) & state if they had dreamed or not
  • If yes, they had to speak about the content
  • Sometimes an experimenter would enter the room and ask them more questions

(GENERAL RESULTS)

  • Uninterrupted dream stages lasted about 3-50 minutes with an estimate average of 20 minutes
  • Dream stages are usually longer later in the night
  • Dream stages has irregular bursts of 2-100 rapid eye movements

(AIM 1: To test whether dream recall differs between REM & nREM sleep)

Design: Natural experiment in a laboratory setting

  • IV: REM or nREM sleep stage
  • DV: Whether participants reported a dream (if so, the details)

Method:

  • Participants were woken either from REM or nREM, but weren't told which stage they were in
  • They confirmed whether they had a dream (if so, reported the content into a recorder)

Results: YES - Dream recall is significantly differs between REM & nREM

  • There were more dreams recalled in REM compared to NREM sleep
  • REM wakenings: 152/191 (79.6%) reported dreaming
  • NREM wakenings: only 11/160 (6.9%) reported dreaming
  • KK had more dream recall in REM than nREM where they recalled zero dreams
  • WD had the highest recall level in REM compared to the other participants

(AIM 2: To investigate whether their is a positive correletion between subjective estimates of dream duration & the length of REM period)

Design: The data was used in both experimental & correlational design

  • Experimental analysis
    • IV: Waking after 5 or 15 minutes of REM sleep
    • DV: Participant's choice
  • Correlational analysis: 2 co-variables
    • Participant's time estimate
    • Number of words in the dream narrative

Method:

  • Participants were woken following 5 or 15 minutes of REM sleep
  • They were asked if they thought they had been dreaming for 5 or 15 minutes
  • Their dream narrative was recorded & the number of words was counted

Researchers changed the test since participants were not accurate enough at estimating their dream duration

Results: YES - There is a positive correlation (r = 0.4 - 0.71)

  • Participants were less likely to estimate 15 mins correctly compared to 5 mins correctly
  • Woken after 5 minutes of dreaming: 88% accuracy
  • Woken after 15 minutes of dreaming: 78% accuracy

(AIM 3: To test whether eye movement patterns are related to dream content)

Design: Natural experiment in a laboratory setting

  • IV: Eye-movement pattern type - this couldn't be manipulated
    • Only horizontal
    • Only vertical
    • Both horizontal & vertical
    • Little or none
  • DV: Report of dream content

Method:

  • Participants were woken after exhibiting a single eye-movement pattern for longer than a minute
  • This was measured using electrodes & an EOG
  • The dream content was recorded

Results: YES - Eye movement patterns are related to dream content

  • Only horizontal (1 dream): climbing up a ladder & looking down
  • Only vertical (3 dreams): watching 2 people throwing tomatoes at each other
  • Both vertical & horizontal (21 dreams): talking to a group of people
  • Little or none (10 dreams): watching something in distance
Conclusions
  • REM sleep is strongly associated with dreaming, as dream recall was much higher during REM than NREM sleep
  • The length of REM periods corresponded to how long participants felt they had been dreaming
  • Eye movements during sleep do correspond with the content of a dream
  • Dreams are not instant events but are reported/experienced in real time
  • The study confirmed that sleep follows a structured, recurring pattern of REM and NREM stages throughout the night
Ethical considerations
  • Participants were identified by their initials to maintain confidentiality
  • Deception - Participant WD was misled about whether he was woken in the REM or nREM stage
Applications
  • The findings can be used to help people with sleep disorders (1 mark: what). Patients can be monitored with an EEG to detect abnormal brain wave patterns during sleep, allowing doctors to identify and treat the disorder (1 mark: how).
  • The findings can be applied to dream analysis therapy (1 mark: what). Patients can be woken during REM sleep and immediately asked to recall their dreams, providing richer material for therapeutic analysis (1 mark: how).

(How does Dement & Kleitman (1957) support biology assumption?)

Participants woken during REM sleep accurately reported dreaming significantly more often than those woken during non-REM sleep (1 mark). This supports the biological assumption that behaviour can be explained through brain function, as dreaming was directly linked to a specific biological stage of sleep (REM), showing that internal brain activity determines psychological experiences such as dreaming (1 mark).

✓ Strengths
  • Objective physiological measures (EEG) → high internal validity
  • Controlled lab setting → extraneous variables minimised
  • Standardised procedure (replicable) → high reliability
  • Used both experimental and correlational methods → triangulation
✗ Weaknesses
  • Small sample (9 participants) → low generalisability
  • The stress of being in a laboratory might affect dream content → low internal validity
  • People used to caffeine may sleep and dream differently without it → low internal validity
  • Do not know if they are telling the truth about dream content → low internal validity
  • Lab setting (artificiality may affect normal sleep patterns; uncomfortable) → low mundane realism & low ecological validity
  • Self-report of dreams: subjective, memory may be inaccurate - low reliability
Hassett et al. (2008) — Monkey Toy Preferences
Field experiment - Independent measures design Observation Opportunity sampling 135 - 82 (21M/61F) - 34 (11M/23F) [rhesus monkey] 3mo-13yo Yerks primate research station (USA) 2008
▼
Background

Hassett et al. argued that biology and socialization interact rather than act separately. Using rhesus monkeys — where gendered socialization is unlikely — they aimed to isolate biological influences, particularly prenatal hormone exposure, on toy preferences, suggesting individuals actively shape their own development rather than passively receiving environmental influences.

Psychology being investigated
  • Sex differences refer to behavioural differences based on biological differences between male and female. These can be based on hormones/chromosomes.
  • Socialisation is any mechanism that changes/adapts a behaviour to societal/cultural expectations.
  • Play any mental/physical activity performed in leisure time for enjoyment/pleasure.
  • The main male hormone is testosterone.
  • The main female hormone is oestrogen.
  • The study looked at whether male and female monkeys preferred masculine or feminine toys
Aim

1. To investigate if sex differences in children’s toy preferences are biologically based rather than learned via socialisation

2. To investigate if male and female rhesus monkeys show sex-based toy preferences similar to human children, but without cultural gender socialization

Design

IV: Gender of monkeys; monkey's rank on social hierarchy

DV: Frequency & duration of play with each toy type

Sample
  • 3 months ⟶ 13 years old
  • Living in natal group at the Yerks primate research station (USA)
  • Initial: 135 rhesus monkeys
  • Exclude: 53 rhesus monkeys
    • 14 monkeys that have received hormone treatments
    • 39 young infants (couldn’t be reliably identified)
  • Experimental sample: 82 rhesus monkeys (61 females, 21 males)
  • Analysis (final sample): 34 rhesus monkeys (23 females, 11 males)
    • Those with less than 5 total recorded behaviours weren’t used in the analysis (3 females & 14 males)
    • 17 rhesus monkeys were excluded during analysis
Materials
  • 25x25m outdoor area & temp-controlled indoor area
  • Toys
    • Girls: 7 plush toys (teddy bear, koala hand puppet, ...) - feminine toys
    • Boys: 6 wheeled toys (wagon, truck, car, shopping cart, ...) - masculine toys
  • 2 video cameras
Procedure
  • Natal group housed in a 25x25m outdoor area with access to a temperature-controlled indoor space
  • 7 trials per group, each lasting 25 minutes
  • One plush toy and one wheeled toy placed 10m apart outdoors (positions counterbalanced)
  • Toys were replaced after each trial - reducing the likelihood of habituation or boredom

Background

Doodling is commonly seen as a distraction, but research suggests it may actually help maintain concentration during boring tasks. While daydreaming is linked to boredom, doodling could serve as a tool to sustain arousal and focus. Using a dual-task design, researchers aimed to better understand how doodling interacts with cognitive resources.

Psychology being investigated

1. To investigate whether doodling while listening to a boring phone message would improve memory (monitoring task) compared to not doodling

2. To investigate if doodling affects the recall of places and names

3. To test the effects of doodling on a boring or mundane task

Design

IV: Doodling group & Non-doodling (control) group

DV: Memory & attention performance (measured quantitatively by calculating the number of correct names and places recalled, minus any false alarms)

Sample
  • 40 members of MRC Applied Psychology Unit participant panel (University of Plymouth, UK)
  • 18-55 years old
  • General population
  • Paid for participating

  • 20 participants
    • 17 females
    • 3 males

(control group)

  • 20 participants
    • 18 females
    • 2 males

  • recorded onto audio cassette tape
  • fairly monotone voice
  • average speaking rate of 227 words per minute
  • played at a comfortable listening volume

  • 8 names of people attending a party
  • names of 3 people & 1 cat who couldn’t attend
  • 8 place names

Background

The original Eyes Test (1997) was developed to measure theory of mind in adults, requiring participants to identify what a person was thinking or feeling from a photo of their eyes. However, the original test had several flaws — it only offered two answer choices used more female than male faces, included emotions that were too easy to identify, and allowed participants to guess based on gaze direction. This led to ceiling effects, where scores were too high to meaningfully distinguish between participants. These limitations prompted a revised, more challenging version of the test.

  • Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects a person’s social and communication skills. Austic individuals misunderstand eye contact, facial expressions, body language, and gestures.
  • High-Functioning Autism (HFA) is a type of autism, considered a milder form, where individuals can still function well in society and cope with daily activities.
  • Asperger’s Syndrome (AS) is a neurodevelopmental disorder on the autism spectrum that affects a person’s social and communication skills, and is characterized by difficulties with social interaction, understanding non-verbal cues, and repetitive behaviors.
  • Theory of mind refers to the ability to understand that other people have their own thoughts, beliefs, and feelings that may be different from your own.
  • Social sensitivity refers to the ability to interpret nonverbal cues, specifically those conveyed through the eyes.
  • Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) is a self-report questionnaire that measured the degree to which individuals possess autistic traits. The test had 50 statements where participants had to agree or disagree. Higher scores suggest the person has more autistic traits. (Theory of mind)

Theory of Mind refers to our ability to attribute mental states — such as thoughts, feelings, and intentions — to ourselves and others (1 mark). It is linked to empathy, which is the ability to understand how other people are feeling or thinking by imagining ourselves in their position (1 mark). We use this knowledge to both explain and predict the actions of others, and to understand that people may hold different emotions and beliefs to our own (1 mark). In Baron-Cohen's study, this was tested using the Eyes Test, where participants had to judge the emotions of others by looking at the eye region of a face only (1 mark — example).

Aim

1. To test if the revised version of the Read the Mind in the Eyes Test would be successful at differentiating participants with AS or HFA (High Functioning Autism) from the general population.

2. To test if there is an inverse (negative) correlation between performance on the revised Eyes Test and scores on the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) in a sample of normal adults.

3. To test if non-autistic females would score higher on the revised Eyes Test than non-autistic males.

  • The AS/HFA group would score significantly lower on the mental state judgements on the Eyes Test, but be unimpaired on the gender control judgements
  • The AS/HFA group would score significantly higher in the AQ
  • Females in the “normal” groups (2&3) would score higher than males on the Eyes Test
  • Males in the “normal” group (3) would score higher than females on the AQ
  • Scores on the AQ & the Eyes Test would be inversely correlated

The study aimed to identify at what point children's eyewitness abilities become comparable to those of adults, with important implications for how child testimony is treated in legal contexts.

Psychology being investigated
  • Eyewitness testimony - when a person attempts to recall what they have seen in relation to a crime (e.g. appearance, clothing) which may be used in court
  • False positive responses - when an eyewitness selects someone from a lineup who is not the actual culprit, but believes their choice is correct
  • Social demands - when people/children feel pressured to make a choice (e.g. children were asked to choose a cartoon face from a line–up)
Aim

1. To investigate the role of social and cognitive factors in children’s identification accuracy in lineups.

2. To assess whether children are less accurate and more prone to false positives than adults.

Design

(Independent measures design)

IV: Age - (children vs adults)

(Repeated measures design)

IV: Line-up type - (target-present vs target-absent)

IV: Familiarity of target - (cartoon vs human)

Sample

(Children)

  • 59 participants (21 females, 38 males)
  • 4-7 years old
  • Recruited from pre-kindergarten & kindergarten classes of 3 private schools in Eastern Ontario, Canada

(Adults)

  • 53 participants (36 females, 17 males)
  • 17-30 years old
  • Recruited from the Introductory Psychology Participant Pool at Eastern Ontario University
Hypothesis
  • Children will be as good as adults at identifying cartoon faces in a target-present line-up
  • Children will be worse than adults at rejecting cartoon faces in a target-absent line-up
  • Children will be worse than adults at identifying human faces in a target-present line-up
  • Children will be worse than adults at rejecting human faces in a target-absent line-up
Materials
  • 4 video clips (2 human, 2 cartoon)
  • 4 photo arrays (present: 1 target + 5 foils, absent: 6 foils) - the foils were rated by 3 judges
  • Black silhouette box for “target not present”
  • Laptop screen to present videos and photo arrays

(how cartoon foils were selected)

  • They were selected from readily available images on the internet
  • They were chosen as they had a similar appearance to Dora/Diego
  • Similarity was based on facial structure/hair length/hair colour
  • 3 judges conducted the ratings on 10 photographs for each target
  • The four cartoon characters receiving the highest similarity scores were used
Procedure
  • Participants told target may or may not be present (selected a silhouette box if absent)
  • Watched 4 clips (6s each, with colour & no sound): 2 humans + 2 cartoons, each with face close-up for 2-3s
    • HUMAN: Caucasian students
      • Male: putting his coat on
      • Female (22 years old): brushing her hair
    • CARTOON: Dora the Explorer & Go, Diego, Go!
      • Dora: talking to the audience
      • Diego: putting on a pair of gloves for safety
  • After each clip, completed free recall task (delay to reduce immediate recall)
  • Shown simultaneous lineup (4 images) on laptop; positions randomised/counterbalanced
  • Lineups: target-present (1 target + 3 foils) or target-absent (4 foils)
  • Foils matched on facial features; images cropped to face/neck/shoulders
  • Responses recorded as "correct identification", "correct rejection", "false identification" or "false rejection"

(Line-up presentation for the target-present condition)

  • The actual target was included in the line-up
  • There were four photographs in the line-up (target + three foils)
  • A silhouette was also in the line-up (to represent the possibility of an absent target)
  • All pictures were shown simultaneously
  • The target's position was randomised & counterbalanced across line-ups
  • Each participant watched four videos presented on a screen in a random order
  • The photo array was shown after each video
  • None of the foils had been in the video

(Line-up presentation for the target-absent condition)

  • The target was replaced with a similar foil/the foil had similar features to the target
  • The target was not in the line-up
  • There were four foils in the line-up
  • A silhouette was also in the line-up (to represent the possibility of an absent target)
  • All pictures were shown simultaneously
  • The target and target replacement were always in the same position
  • The target's position was randomised & counterbalanced across line-ups
  • Each participant watched four videos presented on a screen in a random order
  • The photo array was shown after each video
  • None of the four foils had been in the video
Results
  • Responses to cartoons were generally more accurate than responses to human targets
  • Identifying CARTOON faces: Children (Dora 100%, Diego 97% - 99%) vs Adults (Dora 100%, Diego 89% - 95%)
  • Rejecting CARTOON faces: Children (Dora 80%, Diego 67% - 74%) vs Adults (Dora 96%, Diego 92% - 94%)
  • Identifying HUMAN faces: Children (Female 24%, Male 21% - 23%) vs Adults (Female 46%, Male 85% - 66%)
  • Rejecting HUMAN faces: Children (Female 47%, Male 43% - 45%) vs Adults (Female 72%, Male 67% - 70%)
Conclusions
  • Children are more susceptible to making false identifications in target-absent line-ups
  • Children have a "yes" bias — they feel pressure to pick someone
  • Real-world implication: caution needed when using children as eyewitnesses in court
  • Line-up procedures may need to be adapted for child witnesses
Ethical considerations
  • Informed consent given (from parents for children participants)
  • Have right to withdraw
  • Child were tested individually & monitored for signs of fatigue, anxiety, stress - protection from psychological harm
Applications

1. In Legal/Court Settings: Pozzulo found that children had significantly higher false identification rates than adults. This suggests that child eyewitness testimony should be treated with caution in court, and judges/lawyers should be aware that children are more likely to make incorrect identifications, especially from human lineups.

2. In Police Procedures: Police could use Pozzulo's findings to improve lineup procedures for child witnesses, such as using better instructions that reduce social demand pressure — reminding children that it is acceptable to say the target is not there.

(How does Pozzulo et al. (2011) support cognitive assumption?)

Child witnesses had a higher false identification rate than adults when identifying a culprit from a lineup (1 mark). This shows that children and adults differ in how they cognitively process visual information — when shown a lineup (input), children struggled to accurately process facial features and produce a correct identification (output), suggesting differences in cognitive processing abilities between age groups (1 mark).

  • Standardized procedure = easy to replicate - high reliability
  • Using both qualitative & quantitative data - high internal validity
  • Counterbalancing & randomization: reduced order effects & position bias = results more reliable - high internal validity
  • High ethical procedure

  • Artificial lab setting - low ecological validity
  • Limited age range & diversity of sample (children outside age range & not studied at private school might perform different) - low generalisability

These factors could affect their cognitive abilities, social compliance, and exposure to media, potentially influencing their performance on the identification task.)

  • Potential of demand characteristics (repeated measures design & pressure from authority figures)

Bandura et al. (1961) — Aggression
Lab experiment - Independent measures design, Matched-pairs design Observation Opportunity sampling 72 (36M,36F) 3-6yo Stanford University Nursery School 1961
▼
Background

In the 1960s, behaviourists believed behaviour was learned through rewards and punishments, while Freudian psychoanalysts argued that watching aggression reduced aggressive feelings (catharsis). Bandura challenged both, proposing that children could learn aggression simply by observing others. The Bobo doll experiment was designed to test whether children would imitate an adult's aggressive behaviour without any direct reward or punishment.

Psychology being investigated
  • Aggression is any behaviour intended to harm or hurt another person, either physically (e.g. hitting, kicking) or verbally (e.g. shouting, name-calling)
  • Mallet aggression is any behaviour intended to harm or hurt another person physically using a mallet
  • Social learning theory states that behaviour is learned through observation, imitation, and modelling others
Aim

To investigate whether children would imitate aggressive behaviour modelled by an adult, and whether the sex of the model and the child would affect imitation.

Design

Matched pairs design - children were pre-tested for baseline aggression and then matched in pairs based on similar aggression levels

Independent measures design - children then experienced 3 conditions: aggressive model/non-aggressive model/control

IV: Model behaviour (aggressive, non-aggressive, or none); Sex of model (male/female); Sex of child (boy/girl)

DV: Amount of aggressive behaviour shown by the child (measured by counting specific aggressive actions and remarks)

Inter-rater reliability was tested by having 2 observers independently rate 51 children. Their ratings showed a high correlation (r = 0.89), indicating strong agreement between observers.

Sample
  • 72 children (36 boys, 36 girls)
  • 3-6 years old
  • Enrolled at the Stanford University Nursery School
  • (p/s: a male model & a female model)
Procedure
Children were rated on 4 scales by the experimenter and a nursery school teacher: r = 0.89
Aggressive inhibition
Physical aggression
Verbal aggression
Aggression towards inanimate objects
  • Scores on the 4 aggression scales were added together to give each child a rating
  • Children were arranged into triplets and matched based on their initial aggression ratings
  • One child from each triplet was randomly assigned to one of the 3 conditions:
    • AGGRESSIVE MODEL - 24 children
    • NON-AGGRESSIVE MODEL - 24 children
    • NO MODEL (control condition) - 24 children
  • STAGE 1 (Modelling): Child observed adult model with Bobo doll in the playroom for 10 minutes
    • Things inside the playroom: a small table, a chair, a tinker toy set, a mallet & a 5-foot inflated Bobo doll
    • Aggressive model: sat on, hit with mallet, kicked, punched the bobo doll repeatedly; used distinctive verbal aggression ("Sock him in the nose!" etc.)
    • Non-aggressive: played quietly with other toys
  • STAGE 2 (Aggression Arousal): Child mildly frustrated - told they couldn't play with attractive toys in another room
    • The child was brought to an anteroom containing attractive toys (including a fire engine, locomotive, fighter jet, doll, and spinning top) and told they could play with them.
    • After approximately 2 minutes, the experimenter stopped them and took the toys away, explaining that they were her very best toys reserved for other children.
    • The experimenter then sat away from the child and completed paperwork, leaving the child in a state of mild frustration.
  • STAGE 3 (Test for Delayed Imitation): Child taken to a new room with variety of toys arranged in a fixed order and being observed for 20 minutes through a one-way mirror (since there is no model or experimenter inside the room) - their behaviours was recorded using predetermined categories every 5 seconds
    • Agressive toys: Bobo doll, mallet, dart guns, tether ball, ...
    • Non-agressive toys: tea set, crayons, dolls, cars, farm animals, ...
Results
  • Children who observed aggressive model showed significantly more aggressive behaviour than those in non-aggressive or control conditions
  • Very little difference in aggression recorded between control condition & non-aggressive condition
  • Boys more likely to imitate male model; girls more likely to imitate female model
  • Boys were more physically aggressive than girls
  • Girls were slightly more verbal aggressive
  • Significant difference in levels of imitative aggression between the aggressive condition group vs the other two groups
  • Significant difference in levels of imitative physical aggression vs verbal aggression
Conclusions
  • Witnessing an aggressive act is enough to produce aggression in the observer
  • Children will reproduce aggressive behaviours in the absence of the model
  • Children are more likely to imitate the aggression of a same-sex model
  • Watching violence tended to increase aggressive behavior in children
Ethical considerations
  • Children cannot give informed consent themselves
  • Children were not fully debriefed in a way they could understand after the study
  • Children had no clear right to withdraw from the study
  • Children were exposed to aggressive behaviour - no protection from harm (physically & psychologically)
Applications

1. In Parenting: Bandura's findings suggest that children who witness aggressive behaviour at home are more likely to imitate it. Parents should therefore be aware that their own behaviour acts as a role model, and avoid displaying aggression in front of children to reduce the likelihood of them copying it.

2. In Media/TV Regulations: Since children imitate aggressive models, governments and broadcasters could use Bandura's findings to justify restricting violent content on TV and in video games, particularly content aimed at or accessible to children.

3. In Education: Teachers and schools could apply Bandura's findings by using positive role models in the classroom to encourage prosocial behaviour. If children observe adults and peers behaving kindly and cooperatively, they are more likely to imitate those behaviours.

4. In Therapy/Intervention: Bandura's findings could be used to design intervention programmes for aggressive children, replacing aggressive role models with positive ones to reshape behaviour through observational learning.

(How does Bandura et al. (1961) support learning assumption?)

Children who observed an aggressive model behaving violently towards the Bobo doll were more likely to imitate that aggression themselves (1 mark). This supports the assumption that behaviour is learned through experience, specifically through social learning, where children observed and reproduced the model's behaviour without any direct reinforcement (1 mark).

✓ Strengths
  • Lab experiment: high control, IV clearly operationalised - high internal validity
  • Standardised procedure across conditions = replicable - high reliability
  • Large sample for age group (72 children) - high generalisability/li>
  • Matched pairs design: pre-matched on aggression levels - reduces participant variables
  • High inter-rater reliability (r = 0.89) = results will be more valid
  • Collected both quantitative (objective evidence support hypotheses) & qualitative data (enriched the findings)
✗ Weaknesses
  • Demand characteristics: children may have known "it was OK" to hit Bobo doll - low internal validity
  • Artificial situation: Bobo doll is designed to be hit - low ecological validity
  • Specific sample (Stanford nursery, 3-6 years old) - limited generalisability
  • Measured only short-term aggressive behaviors directed at a doll - providing a limited view of aggression
Fagen et al. (2014) — Elephant Learning
Observation - with behavioural checklist Opportunity sampling 5F [elephant] [4] 5-7yo & [1] 50yo Nepal 2014
▼
Background

Traditional elephant training in Nepal relied heavily on punishment-based techniques. However, there has been a global shift towards more humane methods using positive reinforcement. Fagen et al. investigated whether secondary positive reinforcement (SPR) could train captive elephants to voluntarily participate in trunk washes for tuberculosis (TB) testing — an important procedure as TB can be transmitted between elephants and humans.

Psychology being investigated
  • Operant conditioning is a type of learning where behavior is shaped by rewards (more likely to repeat) or punishments (less likely to repeat)
  • Behavioural chaining is a training technique where individual behaviours are linked together in a sequence, so that completing one behaviour acts as a cue for the next. The reward is only given after the entire sequence is completed correctly.
  • Reinforcement:
    • Positive Reinforcement: Adding a pleasant stimulus after a behaviour to encourage repetition. e.g. giving a banana for correct performance.
    • Negative Reinforcement: Removing an unpleasant stimulus after a behaviour to encourage repetition. e.g. stopping a loud noise when correct behaviour is shown.
    • Primary Reinforcer: The rewarding that fulfils a biological need. e.g. bananas in Fagen et al.
    • Secondary Reinforcer: Can be used as an exchange for a primary reinforcer. e.g. the whistle in Fagen et al. (consistently followed by a primary reinforcer)
Aim

To investigate whether secondary positive reinforcement (SPR) techniques could effectively train traditionally trained elephants in Nepal to voluntarily participate in trunk wash procedures.

Design

IV: Implementation of SPR training

DV: Session duration, number of correct responses, success rate

(Observation techniques)

  • Controlled - captive environment
  • Structured - behavioural checklist

(behavioural checklist)

1. TRUNK HERE: Elephant places the tip of its trunk onto the trainer's open palm

2. TRUNK UP: Elephant holds its trunk upwards in a curl or straight up

3. BUCKET: Elephant gently places its trunk inside a bucket

4. BLOW: Elephant gives a strong exhale through its trunk

5. STEADY: Elephant keeps its trunk still in the required position

Sample
  • 5 female elephants
    • 4 juveniles (5-7 years old)
    • 1 adult (around 50 years old)
  • From Nepal
  • All docile
  • Not currently pregnant
  • Their mahout was willing to participate
  • Traditionally trained
  • No previous experience with SPR training
Procedure
  • Chopped bananas = primary reinforcer
  • Whistle = secondary reinforcer
  • Mahouts present for safety but did not interact during training
  • Elephants offered water before sessions to prevent drinking saline solution
  • Training used:
    • Capturing - Waiting for the elephant to naturally perform a behaviour, then immediately rewarding it. e.g. waiting for the elephant to exhale naturally, then rewarding it to train the "blow" behaviour.
    • Luring - Using a banana to physically guide the elephant into the correct position. e.g. holding banana in palm to encourage "trunk here", or lifting banana above head to encourage "trunk up".
    • Shaping - Rewarding behaviours that are progressively closer to the target behaviour until the full behaviour is achieved. e.g. initially rewarding any exhale towards the bucket, then only rewarding exhales aimed at the centre of the bucket.
    • Verbal cues - Once a behaviour was mastered, a monosyllabic non-word was paired with it as a cue. Non-words were used to avoid mahouts accidentally triggering the cue in everyday conversation.
    • Behavioural chaining - Linking all 5 behaviours into a complete sequence. Started with short sequences (e.g. bucket + blow), then gradually expanded until elephants could perform the full 5 trunk-wash behaviours in the correct order.
  • Syringe introduced gradually via desensitization and counter-conditioning
    • Desensitization = Gradually exposing the elephants to the syringe in small steps to reduce fear — starting by touching it to the outside of the trunk, then slowly progressing to inserting it into the nostril.
    • Counter-conditioning = Pairing the syringe with a banana reward each time it was presented, so the elephants learned to associate the syringe with something positive rather than something threatening.
  • Fluid increased from 1 drop → 15ml → 60ml

How elephants were trained to perform 'trunk up'?

  • Used lure and shaping techniques
  • A banana (primary reinforcer) was placed strategically a few inches from the elephant's forehead
  • The elephant could only reach the banana by lifting its trunk up
  • If the elephant was too big/adult, the lure was held at the end of the trainer's arm
  • The position of the trunk was shaped using increasing height until 'trunk up' was achieved
  • A whistle (secondary reinforcer) was also used
Results
  • 4 juvenile elephants learned full trunk wash in 25-35 sessions
  • Session duration ranged from 10-13 minutes (average: 12 minutes)
  • Elephant 5 (the oldest elephant) never tested for the full trunk wash as she failed to learn the full sequence
  • Success rate of an individual behaviours increased from 39% (session 10) to 89.3% (session 35)

Saavedra & Silverman (2002) — Button Phobia (Koumpounophobia)
Case study Observation Self-report - Interview Opportunity sampling 1 boy 9yo Hispanic-American Child Anxiety and Phobic Program 2002
▼
Background

Classical conditioning (e.g. Little Albert) explained how phobias are learned through association, but couldn't fully explain disgust-based reactions. Evidence from blood-injury and spider phobia studies suggested targeting disgust could reduce symptoms. This led Saavedra and Silverman to explore evaluative learning as an alternative mechanism, and to incorporate systematic desensitisation (gradual exposure) into their treatment approach.

Role of disgust in childhood phobias:

  • Disgust = feeling of aversion towards something feared → leads to avoidance behaviour
  • Children can negatively evaluate a phobic object without anticipating any threat
  • Since there is no fear, it is disgust causing the negative evaluation
  • A study on spider phobia showed disgust decreased as fear decreased — showing a link between the two
  • Children can be repulsed by a previously neutral stimulus
  • Usually triggered by a traumatic event in childhood
Psychology being investigated
  • Evaluative learning is a psychological process where a person develops a positive or negative feeling toward something because it becomes associated with another emotional experience.
  • Operant conditioning is learning through consequences — behaviours that are rewarded are more likely to be repeated, and behaviours that are punished are less likely to be repeated.
  • Classical conditioning is learning through association — a neutral stimulus becomes associated with an unconditioned stimulus until it produces the same response on its own.
  • Phobias is an intense, irrational, and persistent fear of a specific object, situation, or activity that causes significant distress and avoidance behaviour.
Aim

To investigate the role of classical conditioning and the role of fear and disgust in relation to a button phobia of a boy.

Design

Case study design → gathers in-depth quantitative & qualitative data

  • Quantitative data: Distress ratings / Severity ratings
  • Qualitative data: Questions about why does the boy found buttons disgusting
Sample
  • A 9-year-old Hispanic-American boy
  • He was presented (by his mum) to a Child Anxiety and Phobic Program
  • He did not meet the criteria for OCD
  • He did meet the criteria (DSM-IV) for a phobia
  • He had experienced a bowl of buttons falling on him
  • He had his phobia at 5 years of age (4 years prior to study)
  • Origin of his phobia:
    • The phobia began when the boy was 5 years old in kindergarten, during an art project involving buttons
    • The boy ran out of buttons, so he was asked to come to the front of the class & take them. His hand slipped when he reached the bowl & the buttons fell on him. He described this experience as distressful.
    • During the 4 years after the button incident, the boy did not experience or report any major stressful events that could explain why the phobia started
Procedure

(Diagnosis)

  • The child and parent were interviewed about the phobia
  • The boy met the DSM-IV criteria for a specific phobia of buttons
  • The boy’s presentation of symptoms did not meet the criteria for OCD

(Feelings Thermometer)

  • A hierarchy of distress/fear/disgust
  • Measured the boy's subjective feelings of distress about different types of buttons
  • 9-point scale (0–8): 0 = no distress, 8 = highest distress
  • 11 different buttons were rated
  • Large denim buttons = least distressing (rated 2)
  • Small clear plastic buttons = most distressing (rated 8)

Phase 1 — Behavioural Exposure (4 sessions)

  • Exposure-based treatment targeting both thoughts (cognitions) & behaviour
  • Gradually exposed to real buttons from least (large denim) → most distressing (small clear plastic)
  • Sessions: 30 mins (the boy alone), then 20 mins (the boy & his mother)
  • Mother provided positive reinforcement after each completed task

Phase 2 — Imagery Exposure & Cognitive Restructuring (7 sessions)

  • Planned 7 additional sessions after behavioural exposure to address the boy’s disgust-related thoughts & imagery
  • Sessions focused on exploring what he found disgusting about buttons using cognitive strategies
  • Cognitive restructuring used to challenge negative thoughts about buttons
  • Boy imagined increasingly distressing button-related scenarios:
    • Hundreds of buttons falling on him
    • Hugging his mother wearing many buttons
  • He was asked to describe how they looked, felt, smelled & how this made him feel
  • The boy described buttons as 'disgusting & gross', reporting they felt unpleasant on his body and gave off unpleasant smells, yet struggled to explain why

(Follow-up)

Assessed at 6 and 12 months after treatment using Feelings Thermometer and Interviews

Results

Phase 1 — Behavioural Exposure

  • Boy completed all exposure tasks
  • However, distress ratings increased between sessions 2–4

    • (severity ratings)

    • Remained the same for the first 2 sessions
    • Increased from session 2 to session 3
    • Increased from session 3 to session 4
  • Suggested behavioural exposure addressed avoidance but failed to reduce underlying disgust

Phase 2 — Imagery Exposure & Cognitive Restructuring

  • Distress ratings decreased across sessions:
    • Hundreds of buttons falling on him (distress: 8 → 5 → 3)
    • Hugging his mother wearing many buttons (distress: 7 → 4 → 3)
  • Boy described buttons as "disgusting and gross" and reported they gave off unpleasant smells — highlighting disgust rather than fear as the key emotion

Post-treatment session (6 & 12 months)

  • Reported minimal distress about buttons
  • No longer met DSM-IV criteria for specific phobia
  • Could wear clothes with plastic buttons including his school uniform without distress
Conclusions
  • Button phobia involved both Expectancy Learning (fear) & Evaluative Learning (disgust)
  • Treatment targeting both fear & disgust responses was effective
  • Classical conditioning principles can explain & treat specific phobias
Ethical considerations
  • The boy & his mother both provided informed consent to participate in the study
Applications

After just 4 sessions of the mother providing positive reinforcement, the boy could cope with his worst button fear. Therfore, for children with phobias, having a parents involved in the therapy might bring about faster positive outcomes.

(How does Saavedra & Silverman (2002) support learning assumption?)

The boy was not born with his button phobia — it developed after a bowl of buttons fell on him (1 mark). This supports the assumption that behaviour is learned through experience, specifically through classical conditioning, where the buttons became associated with a frightening event, leading to a phobia (1 mark).

✓ Strengths
  • Rich, detailed data from multiple techniques (triangulation)
  • Documents rare phobia — unique contribution to knowledge
  • Follow-up data shows treatment maintained over time
  • Multiple measures (behavioural, self-report, physiological ratings)
✗ Weaknesses
  • Single case: cannot generalise to others with button phobia
  • Multiple treatment elements: can't identify which worked
  • Self-report scales: subjective, child may under/over-report
Similarities & Differences (Bandura et al. vs Fagen et al. vs Saavedra & Silverman)

▼

(Fagen et al. vs Bandura et al.)

Similarity

Both the Fagen study and the Bandura study used observation as part of their data collection techniques. This means that specific behaviours could be operationalised to improve validity. For example, in Fagen, "trunk down" meant the trunk was hanging loose, and in Bandura, "gun play" meant pretending to shoot at an object in the room.

Difference

The two studies differed in their sample. Fagen used 5 female elephants, whereas Bandura used 72 children. This means Bandura's study has greater generalisability than Fagen's, as the larger and more varied sample is more representative, making it easier to apply the findings to a wider population.

(Saavedra & Silverman vs Bandura et al.)

Similarity

This improves the validity of both studies as the data is less open to interpretation.

The two studies focus on different theories from the learning approach. Bandura focused on social learning — children learned aggression through imitation and observation of a model. This means Bandura is about vicarious learning, whereas Saavedra is about direct experience.

Milgram (1963) — Obedience
Observation (Lab setting) Self-report - Interview Volunteer sampling 40M 20-50yo New Haven (USA) 1963

Background

Milgram originally planned to compare obedience levels between Americans and Germans, suspecting German culture explained Nazi atrocities. However, American participants at Yale were already so unexpectedly obedient that he abandoned the cross-cultural comparison entirely — suggesting obedience to authority is a universal human tendency, not a cultural one.

14 psychology students were asked to:

  • Predict the behaviour of 100 hypothetical people
  • Had to plot the distribution of obedience based on the characteristics of the sample
  • Predict the percentage of participants would press the 450V switch (0-3%)
Psychology being investigated
  • Obedience is the act of following order given by a figure in authority
  • Destructive obedience means following orders from an authority figure even when doing so causes harm to others
  • Social pressure is the influence exerted by a group or society on an individual to conform or comply with expected norms, behaviours, or demands
Aim

To investigate how far ordinary people would obey an authority figure, even when doing so conflicted with their personal conscience and involved harming another person.

Design

Laboratory setting (no IV & no control condition) ⟶ CONTROLLED OBSERVATION

Measure: Levels of obedience (operationalized as the maximum voltage given in response to the orders)

Sample

Participants (Teacher)

  • 40 males
  • 20-50 years old
  • New Haven district of Connecticut, USA
  • Wide range of occupations & educational levels:

    • 22.5% professional

  • Stern & emotionless throughout the experiment

Mr. Wallace (Learner - confederate)

  • Held at Yale University to appear legitimate

  • Individuals appear to be much more obdient to an authority figure than we might expect
  • People find it stressful to follow destructive orders from a person in authority
Ethical considerations
  • No protection from psychological harm ⟶ participants would not exit the study in the same mental state they entered
  • Deception about the aim of the study & whether the shocks were real = lack of fully informed consent
  • Participants were inadequate debriefing
  • Right to withdraw might not have been there as prods were used
Application

1. Military training: Soldiers could be encouraged to be more obedient as an authority figure give out commands in a firm-toned voice.

2. Classroom obedience: A teacher could get student to be more obedient in the classroom by wearing clothes that show authority like a technician's coat.

Oxytocin affects the amygdala, which regulates preferred interpersonal distance. Rather than being a simple "pro-social" hormone, Perry et al. tested the social salience hypothesis — the idea that oxytocin amplifies existing social cues rather than making everyone more sociable. This means oxytocin can make someone feel more at ease or more threatened depending on the context, with effects varying based on individual differences in empathy.

  • Interpersonal distance (personal space) is the physical space between people during social interactions, which varies depending on the relationship and context.
  • Social hormones are hormones such as oxytocin that influence social behaviour and interactions between individuals.
  • Empathy is the ability to understand the emotional state of someone else by seeing it from another person's perspective.

Piliavin et al. (1969) — Subway Samaritans
Field experiment Observation Opportunity sampling ~4450 New York subway 1969
▼
Background

After the Kitty Genovese murder (1964), Darley & Latané introduced bystander apathy — people are less likely to help in emergencies when others are present (diffusion of responsibility). Lab studies had shown this, but this study tested it in a naturalistic, real-world setting.

Psychology being investigated
  • Bystander apathy is when people witness an event (like a crime) are less likely to intervene/ give help.
  • Diffusion of responsibility is when each bystander feels less personally responsible when others are present.
  • Cost-reward model is the idea that before helping, bystanders weigh up the costs (e.g. effort, risk) and rewards (e.g. satisfaction, gratitude) of helping — and are more likely to help when rewards outweigh costs.
Aim

To investigate how people react to someone collapsing on a train, specifically looking at: type of victim (ill vs drunk), race of victim, behaviour of model, and group size.

Design

Setting: New York City subway carriages (7½ minute journey)

IV:

  • Type of victim (drunk / ill)
  • Race of victim (black / white)
  • Model behaviour (close / far from victim & helped early / late)
  • Group size (naturally occurring number of passengers present)

DV - level of bystander helping, operationalised as:

  • Time taken for the first passenger to help
  • Total number of passengers who helped
  • Race, gender, and location of each helper also recorded
  • Qualitative data = verbal remarks made by passengers
Sample

Passengers (participants)

  • Subway between Harlem and the Bronx, weekdays 11am–3pm
  • Total estimated ~4450 passengers
  • 45% black, 55% white
  • Mean passengers per carriage = 43
  • Mean passengers in critical area = 8.5

Victims (confederates)

  • Males aged 26–35, dressed identically (jacket, old trousers, no tie)
  • 3 white, 1 black
  • Drunk condition (38 trials): smelled of alcohol, alcohol bottle was wrapped in brown paper bag
  • Ill condition (65 trials): acted sober, carried a black cane

Models (confederates)

  • All white males aged 24–29
  • Dressed informally
  • When helping: raised victim to sitting position and stayed until next stop

(Model conditions)

Procedure
  • 4 teams of student researchers (2 male + 2 female per team)
  • Female confederates sat in adjacent area — observed and recorded data covertly
  • Male confederates played victim and model roles
    • Victim: stand at the pole at the center of critical area
    • Model: remain standing throughout the trial

Results

(Overall)

  • 78% of victims received spontaneous help (before model intervened)
  • In 60% of cases, more than one person helped
  • Helping rates were much higher than previous laboratory studies

(Type of victim)

  • Ill victim: helped in 95% of trials (62/65); usually within 70 seconds
  • Drunk victim: helped in only 50% of trials (19/38)
  • Ill victims usually received help faster than drunk victims

(Race)

  • Black and white cane victims equally likely to receive help
  • Drunk condition showed minor same-race helping
  • Black drunk victims received less help overall

(Sex)

  • 90% of first helpers were male
  • Female passengers commented:
    • "It's for men to help him"
    • "I wish I could help him - I'm not strong enough"

(Model effect)

  • Difficult to analyse as most helping was spontaneous
  • Early model intervention (70 seconds) slightly more effective than late (150 seconds)

(Group size)

  • No evidence of diffusion of responsibility
  • Larger groups (7 people) actually responded faster than smaller groups (3 people) — directly opposing the diffusion of responsibility hypothesis

Cost-benefit model:

  • Witnessing emergency raises arousal (disgust, sympathy, courage)
  • Arousal prompts individuals to act to reduce the uncomfortable feeling — either by helping (if benefits > costs) or moving away (if costs > benefits)
  • In ~20% of trials, passengers actively moved away from the critical area instead of helping
  • More verbal comments made during drunk trials and trials without helping
Conclusions
  • In a natural setting, many people offer spontaneous help to a stranger even in a group situation
  • No evidence of diffusion of responsibility
  • Cost-benefit model: helping ill victim = low cost; helping drunk = higher cost (e.g. vomit, embarrassment)

Several factors influence the decision to help:

  • Type of victim — cane victim helped more than drunk victim
  • Gender of helper — men more likely to help than women
  • Similarity to victim — people more likely to help their own race, especially if victim is drunk
  • Duration of emergency — the longer it continues, the less likely help is given, and the more likely people find another way to cope with arousal
Ethical considerations
  • No informed consent — participants did not agree to take part
  • No debrief after the study
  • Deception — participants believed the victim had genuinely collapsed
  • Potential psychological distress — guilt at not helping or concern about the victim's wellbeing
Applications

1. Public health campaigns: Understanding factors that reduce helping (e.g. drunk victim, long duration) can inform campaigns encouraging people to help regardless of the victim's appearance.

2. Charitable giving: Cost-benefit model can be applied to donation behaviour — reducing the "cost" of donating (e.g. making it easier/quicker) increases likelihood of helping.

This supports the assumption that behaviour is influenced by social context, as the situation and perceived cause of the victim's condition shaped whether bystanders chose to help (1 mark).

(Piliavin et al. vs Perry et al.)

Difference 1

The two studies used different samples. Piliavin used an opportunity sample of approximately 4450 passengers on the New York subway, whereas Perry used a volunteer sample of 54 male undergraduate students at the University of Haifa. The two studies used stooges in different ways. In Piliavin, a human stooge acted as the victim (ill/drunk) and the participant was unaware they were acting. In Perry, participants 'met' an animated stooge in various computer scenarios until they felt it was invading their personal space. This means Piliavin had higher ecological validity as the stooge interaction was real, whereas Perry's animated stooge may not have triggered genuine personal space responses.

Diagnostic criteria

According to ICD-11, there are some core symptoms:

  • Persistent delusions (+) → faulty, strongly held beliefs
  • Persistent hallucinations (+) → hear/see things that are not there
  • Thought disorder (+) → unable to think & speak in an organized manner
  • Experiences of influence, passivity, or control (+) → believe that your thoughts & actions are controlled/influenced by somebody else or something external

Other possible symptoms:

  • Avolition (-) → lack of motivation
  • Flattened affect (-) → blunted emtional expression
  • Impaired cognitive function (-) → reduced memory/attention
  • Catatonia syndrome (-) → lack of movement/speech

ICD-11:The 11th edition of the International Classification of Diseases. This is the global standard for coding health information (both physical & mental health disorders). It was developed & is updated by the World Health Organization (WHO).

Positive symptoms (+):An experience that is 'an addition to' or 'a distortion of' normal experience.

Negative symptoms (-):This occurs when level of functioning or experience falls below normal levels.

🚧 For a diagnosis of schizophrenia to be made, symptoms must have persisted for at least 1 month & must not be due to another health condition (such as brain tumour) or due to the effect of a substance/medication. 🚧

Example study 1: Aneja et al. (2018)

Presented three cases of early-onset schizophrenia, demonstrating that children can experience symptoms such as auditory hallucinations, persecutory delusions, disorganized thinking and speech, social withdrawal, and academic decline, highlighting the need for early diagnosis and intervention.

Early-onset: When a disorder starts at a significantly younger age than average. Schizophrenia typically starts during early adulthood.

Types of delusion
  • Persecutory delusion is a strongly held belief that you are in danger, that you are being conspired against & that others are pursuing you to try to do you harm
  • Grandiose delusion is a strongly held belief that you are someone with special abilities or special powers
  • Delusion of reference is a strongly held belief that events in the environment are related to you

Title

Can virtual reality be used to investigate persecutory ideation?

Context

Research by Rothbaum et al. (2000) shows that people suffering from a range of phobias have been successfully treated using VR (exposure therapy).

Main theories

Persecutory ideation (one of the most common symptoms of schizophrenia) can lead to complete social withdrawal & an inability to maintain usual activities due to fear of hostility from others. However, persecutory ideation is hard for people without schizophrenia to understand due to the complex nature of the disorder. VR could help develop a better understand of persecutory ideation in those with schizophrenia, thus, lead to better ways of helping those with schizophrenia understand & learn to cope with their persecutory ideation.

Aims

  • To investigate whether participants without a history of mental illness have thoughts of a persecutory nature in VR.
  • To investigate whether there are cognitive or emotional factors that predict the likelihood of persecutory ideation being shown in VR.

Design

  • Sample: 12 males, 12 females; University College London, UK; average age of 26 years; no history of mental illness; volunteer sampling.
  • Lab experiment: participants enter a virtual environment (a library scene) for 5 minutes & were asked to explore the environment and try to form an impression of the people in the room (5 avatars with ambiguous behaviour) without being told that the research was investigating persecutory ideation.
  • After leaving the VR environment, participants were given a range of questionaires.
    • BSI - measured interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility & psychoticism.
    • Other questionnaires - measured anxiety, paranoia, 'sense of presence' (the extent to which the participant felt they were a part of the virtual world) & specific ideations of persecution.
    • The questionnaires included closed questions, where participants gave their answer on a numerical scale.
  • Semi-structured interviews were also carried out to find out the participants' thoughts & feelings about the virtual reality experience.

Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI):A 53-item self-report measure designed to assess 9 symptom dimensions (interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility & psychoticism) over the last 7 days.

Results

  • There was a significant correlation between scores on the paranoia questionnaire & the paranoia score given based on the interview.
  • The results showed that most participants had positive opinions about the avatars, but some had ideas of persecution.
  • There were no significant differences in the persecution scores between males & females, or between those who had questionnaires only after, or before and after the task.
  • Persecutory thoughts about the avatars did not significant correlate with paranoia score.
  • Higher levels of interpersonal sensitivity & higher levels of anxiety were significantly correlated with higher levels of persecutory thoughts in VR.

Conclusions

  • People do attribute mental states to VR characters (usually positive but can be persecutory in nature).
  • People are more likely to show persecutory ideation if they show high levels of interpersonal sensitivity or anxiety.

Evaluation

  • The research was ethical in that participants gave consent, had the right to withdraw & were debriefed afterwards.
  • The findings that VR can be used effectively as a measure of persecutory ideation is applicable to everyday life as this suggests VR could be used again to measure similar experiences.
  • This study shows us how people respond to & interpret behaviours & intentions in avatars in VR, but this cannot tell us for certain how people would respond to similar experiences in everyday life.

✓ Strengths
  • Standardized approach (specially designed VR program) — increases reliability of measurement
  • Combination of numerical scales & semi-structured interviews — gather both quantitative data (for data analysis) & qualitative data (for detailed information)
  • Used of control (half the participants completed questionnaires both before & after the VR) — increases the validity
  • Equal split of males & females — no gender bias
✗ Weaknesses
  • Virtual environment — low ecological validity
  • Relies on self-report — response bias
  • Small sample (24) & same university — low generalizability
Explanations

Biological

Genetic: One biological explaination for schizophrenia is that genes or particular combinations of genes are passed on to offspring which may cause the disorder to develop. Three ways of investigating the genetic explanation for schizophrenia are family studies, twin studies & adoption studies.

Family study: A study that investigates whether biological relatives are more likely to share a trait, behaviour, or disorder than people who are not biologically related.

Twin study: A study that compares monozygotic (MZ) and dizygotic (DZ) twins to investigate the role of genetics in traits, behaviours, or disorders.

Adoption study: A study that compares adopted individuals with their biological parents to investigate the role of genetics and environment in traits, behaviours, or disorders.

  • Family studies generally shows that if you have close family members with schizophrenia you are more likely to also develop schizophrenia. This suggests that schizophrenia is at least partly caused by genetics.

    • e.g. Gottesman (1991) found that the likelihood of developing schizophrenia went from 1% in the general population, up to 48% if you have an identical twin with schizophrenia.

  • Twin studies are a very useful way of studying the role of genetics in schizophrenia because they allow researchers to study the relative influences of nature & nurture. This research tends to show that the concordance of schizophrenia is higher in MZ twins than DZ twins, again, suggesting there is a genetic contribution.

    • e.g. Hilker et al. (2017) carried out a twin study with over 30000 twin pairs in Denmark & concluded that heritability of schizophrenia was 79%.

Monozygotic (MZ) twins: Identical twins, who share 100% of their DNA with one another.

Dizygotic (DZ) twins: Non-identical twins, who share approximately 50% of their DNA with one another.

Concordance: The extent to which both individuals in a pair, such as twins or family members, share the same observable trait or disorder.

  • Adoption studies provide stronger evidence for the role of genetics because they truly separate inherited genes from the environment in which a child is raised. This is achieved by comparing a child raised by an adoptive family with their biological parent, who they were not raised by.

    • e.g. Tienari et al. (2000) found schizophrenia in 6.7% of adoptees with a biological mother with schizophrenia, compared to just 4% of a control group.

Biochemical (dopamine hypothesis): The dopamine hypothesis essentially states that the brains of people with schizophrenia produce more dopamine (a type of neurotransmitter) than the brains of people without schizophrenia. It identifies a link between excessive amounts of dopamine/dopamine receptors & positive symptoms of schizophrenia & related disorders. Research suggests that an excess of dopamine in particular brain regions can be related to certain symptoms.

e.g. An increase in dopamine in the Broca's region (responsible for formation of language) can impair logical speech, which is a classic symptom of schizophrenia.

  • Drug trials: People with schizophrenia & people without the disorder both taking drugs that increase the level of dopamine in the brain (include amphetamines & cocaine). Large increases in dopamine production are correlated with an increase in the reporting of hallucinations & delusions. (Lindstrom et al., 1999)
  • Post-mortem studies: Brain fluid from decreased patients had abnormally low levels of the enzyme that breaks down dopamine, suggesting that dopamine may have been present in excessive quantities. (Wise et at., 1974)
  • PET scan: Shows greater dopamine activity (deep purple) in the striatum, limbic system & cortex in the brain of an individual with schizophrenia than one without schizophrenia. Excessive dopamine activity in these areas may be linked to positive symptoms while some reaserch (Nestler, 1997) suggests that decreased dopamine activity in the prefrontal cortex of schizophrenia patients may correlate with negative symptoms.

Neurotransmitter: A chemical messenger that enables communication between the neurons in the brain. To allow the nerve impulse to pass between two cells, the neurotransmitter moves across a small junction known as a 'synapse'.

Drug trial: Research studies where different drugs/medications are given to groups of people & their responses/outcomes are compared.

Post-mortem studies: The examination of a person's brain after they have died, to investigate abnormalities that could explain symptoms/conditions they experienced when they were alive.

Positron emission tomography (PET) scanning: A technique that uses gamma cameras to detect radioactive tracers, such as glucose that is injected into the blood. The tracer accumulates in areas of high activity during the scan, allowing them to become visible for analysis.

Psychological

Cognitive: Schizophrenia is viewed as involving faulty mental processes, rather than relying solely on physiological explanations. Frith (2015) proposed that many symptoms of schizophrenia result from impairments in self-monitoring, generating spontaneous actions, and Theory of Mind.

  • Frith describes schizophrenia as an 'abnormality of self-monitoring'. Individuals fail to recognise that their inner speech and thoughts are self-generated, instead attributing them to an external source. This can lead to positive symptoms such as auditory hallucinations, thought insertion, delusions of alien control, and other delusional beliefs as individuals attempt to make sense of these abnormal experiences.

    • e.g. Failures in self-monitoring can lead to auditory hallucinations, thought insertion, and delusions of alien control.

  • Frith proposed that negative symptoms, particularly avolition, result from an impairment in generating spontaneous actions. Individuals have difficulty initiating behaviour or making decisions independently, although they may still respond appropriately when prompted by external stimuli.

    • e.g. Someone with negative symptoms may find it difficult to start a conversation with somesone else (a spontaneous action) but they may be able to answer questions when asked (responding to a stimulus).

  • Frith also proposed that impaired Theory of Mind (ToM), the inability to accurately understand one's own and others' mental states, is a key cognitive deficit in schizophrenia. This impairment is most strongly associated with persecutory delusions, as individuals may misinterpret other people's intentions as hostile or threatening, and may also contribute to social withdrawal by making social interactions difficult and confusing.
Treatment & management

Biological

Biochemical treatment: During the 1950s, antipsychotics were developed, which work by reducing the activity of dopamine & as a result reducing the symptoms. Antipsychotics are usually the first treatment given after a diagnosis of schizophrenia & often used alongside psychological theraipies such as cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT).

  • Typical antipsychotics (first generation): Developed in the 1950s that reduce the effect of dopamine in order to reduce positive symptoms of schizophrenia. These are dopamine antagonists, which means that they block dopamine receptors so there is less dopamine activity. However, the downside to this is that there are many undesirable side effects.

    • e.g. Chlorpromazine - works mainly by blocking dopamine D2 receptors (especially in the mesolimbic pathway) to reduce positive psychotic symptoms, at the cost of significant extrapyramidal side effects from D2 blockade elsewhere in the brain.

  • Atypical antipsychotics (second generation): Developed in the 1990s that affect dopamine levels in order to reduce both positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia. Compared to typical antipsychotics, atypical antipsychotics have a lower risk of side effects and have been shown to be effective for 'treatment-resistant' patients. These also work by blocking dopamine, but unlike typical antipsychotics, they rapidly dissociate (only block dopamine activity for a short period of time). This rapid dissociation allows for normal dopamine transmission to take place, leads to less side effects.

    • e.g. Clozapine - works through weaker D2 blockade combined with strong serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism, giving it efficacy against both positive and negative symptoms with far fewer motor side effects, but carrying a risk of agranulocytosis.

Diagnostic criteria
  • Mood disorders are characterized by episodes of particular types of mood over time.
  • Mood episodes can include: depressive, manic, mixed, and hypomanic.

Depressive disorder (unipolar)

  • Characterized by depressive mood (feeling sad, irritable, empty) or loss of pleasure.
  • Symptoms: difficulty concentrating, excessive feelings of worthlessness or guilt, recurrent thoughts of death, or changes to eating or sleeping patterns.
  • Diagnosis can only be made if the individual has never experienced a manic, mixed or hypomanic episode.
  • Single episode depressive disorder is characterized by the presence of one depressive episode, without history of previous episodes.
  • Recurrent depressive disorder is diagnosed when there are at least two depressive episodes seperated by several months.

Depressive episode: A period of at least two weeks, which involves depressed mood or lack of interest in usual activities for most of the day, nearly every day.

Bipolar disorders

  • There are two types of bipolar disorder: Type 1 & Type 2.
  • Both are episodic mood disorders, but differ in types of mood episodes experienced.

  • Occurs at least one manic or mixed episode.
  • Manic episode:
    • Lasts at least one week.
    • Characterized by an extreme mood - usually feeling euphoria but also irritable.
    • Symptoms: rapid speech, impulsivity, reckless behaviour, extremely high self-esteem, and rapid changes between mood states.
  • Mixed episode:
    • A period of two weeks.
    • Characterized by a mixture or rapid alternating between manic and depressive states.

Episodic mood disorder: A condition characterized by episodes of time where mood is either very low or very high.

Measures: Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)
  • An example of psychometric testing.
  • 21-item self-report measure.
  • Assesses attitudes and symptoms of depressive disorder.
  • Each item in the inventory consists of at least four statements.
  • The person taking the test must choose the one statement that best fits how they have been feeling during a recent period of time.
  • Total score across the test is used to determine the severity of the disorder.
    • 0-13: Minimal or no depression
    • 14-19: Mild depression
    • 20-28: Moderate depression
    • 29-63: Severe depression
  • Current version BDI-II was issued in 1996 & has been revised to include the symptoms of increase of appetite, loss of appetite and fatigue.
  • High levels of reliability and validity fairly accurate & consistent in measuring the severity of an individual's level of depressive disorder
  • Quantitative measurement provides a numerical score for each person -> objectivity & comparison
  • Quantitative measurements and structured interviews cannot capture the details & in-depth information about reasons behind their answers
  • Self-report risk of low validity as person taking the test may either exaggerate or play down their symptoms
Explanations

Biological

Biochemical:

  • Two of the main neurotransmitters have been identified as being associated with depressive disorder are dopamine and serotonin.
  • Dopamine responsible for feelings of motivation and pleasure.
  • Low levels of dopamine are often found in those suffering from depressive disorder.
  • Anti-depressents reduce symptoms of depressive disorder by increasing dopamine levels.
  • Serotonin regulates sleep and appetite as well as mood and anxiety.
  • Low levels of serotonin lead to low mood, anxiety and disruption to sleeping and eating patterns.
  • Most common anti-depressents are SSRIs - work by increasing serotonin levels.

Genetic:

  • First-degree relatives share 50% of their DNA.
  • Some mental disorders are thought to have a genetic basis, meaning they can be transmitted from one generation to the next.
